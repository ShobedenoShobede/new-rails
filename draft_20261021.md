**Headline**  
U.S. banks double stable‑coin transfers, while global projects race to tokenise real‑world assets and local currencies – and regulators are being pressed for clearer rules.

---

### What Happened  

In the United States, stable‑coin usage surged dramatically over the past month. Coinbase’s latest market report shows that the total value of stable‑coin transfers hit **$891 billion**, a four‑fold increase from a year earlier, even though the overall market‑cap of stable‑coins has barely moved. The bulk of this activity occurs outside normal banking hours, reflecting a growing appetite among corporate treasuries and fintech firms for on‑demand, digital settlement that bypasses legacy clearing cycles. (CoinTelegraph, “Stablecoin transfers quadruple to $891B as US banks expand adoption.”)

At the same time, several blockchain platforms are pushing tokenised‑asset infrastructure that could feed that demand.  

* **Tx** introduced **USTX**, a new stablecoin issued by Brale that can be minted with USDC from eight different networks. The token is explicitly designed to settle tokenised stocks and other real‑world assets on Tx’s chain, positioning the platform as a bridge between fiat‑backed stablecoins and securities tokenisation. (CoinTelegraph, “Tx launches USTX stablecoin to pull RWA liquidity onto its chain.”)  

* **Injective** and **BDACS** announced a plan to launch **KRW1**, a Korean‑won‑backed stablecoin intended for settlement of tokenised assets. The move coincides with South Korea’s draft regulatory framework for local‑currency stablecoins, suggesting that the country is preparing a domestic alternative to dollar‑denominated digital money. (CoinTelegraph, “Injective, BDACS plan Korean won stablecoin settlement for tokenised assets.”)  

* In Southeast Asia, **Bank Negara Malaysia** rolled out a distributed‑ledger‑technology (DLT) platform for tokenised bonds and sukuk. The system links directly to the country’s existing RENTAS+ settlement infrastructure, allowing central‑bank money to settle tokenised securities in real time. (CoinTelegraph, “Malaysia central bank launches DLT platform for tokenised bonds.”)

Conversely, the industry’s expansion faces push‑back. **OKX** and **ICE** have proposed a joint tokenised‑stock platform, but roughly a quarter of the U.S. companies slated for inclusion have formally opted out, citing concerns over governance, market impact and regulatory exposure. High‑profile firms including Alphabet, Microsoft and Goldman Sachs have lodged objections, signalling that the “opt‑out” option may become a powerful tool for issuers. (CoinTelegraph, “Alphabet, Microsoft add objections to OKX‑ICE proposed tokenised stock platform.”)

Finally, the regulatory environment remains uncertain. **Bitwise** and **Hyperliquid** have filed formal comments with the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) seeking clearer guidance on how token‑based assets and airdrops should be classified and traded. Their submissions arrive as the SEC’s public comment period draws to a close, highlighting the industry’s need for a rulebook before scaling further. (CoinTelegraph, “Bitwise, Hyperliquid seek SEC rule changes for token trading, airdrops.”)

---

### The Bigger Picture  

The data points above illustrate a clear shift: **the rail on which value moves is being rebuilt from a paper‑centric, batch‑processed system to a digital, token‑driven network that can settle 24/7.**  

* **Who wins?**  
  * **Corporations and treasuries** that need rapid, cross‑border liquidity will benefit from stable‑coin pipelines that operate outside traditional banking windows. The quadrupling of transfer volume shows that firms are already using these channels for cash‑management, payroll and supply‑chain payments.  
  * **Platform providers** that can combine stable‑coin liquidity with tokenised‑asset settlement (e.g., Tx, Injective) stand to capture a new fee stream, essentially acting as “digital custodians” for securities that were previously only tradable on legacy exchanges.  
  * **Regulators that issue central‑bank money on DLT** (Malaysia) gain a testbed for modernising sovereign debt markets, potentially reducing settlement risk and increasing transparency for investors.

* **Who loses?**  
  * **Traditional clearinghouses and custodians** may see a gradual erosion of their monopoly on post‑trade processing, especially if tokenised securities can settle in minutes rather than days.  
  * **Issuers that refuse tokenisation** could find themselves at a competitive disadvantage. Companies that opt out of platforms like OKX‑ICE risk missing out on a new investor base that prefers digital, fractional ownership and instant settlement.  
  * **Projects without robust security or governance** continue to be weeded out, as evidenced by the 2026 wave of crypto shutdowns that claimed 82 projects. The market is consolidating around platforms that can demonstrate resilience and regulatory compliance.

* **What does this mean for the old system vs. the new one?**  
  The old system—centralised clearing, batch settlement, and reliance on correspondent banking—was built for a world where speed was less critical than safety. Today’s data shows that speed and accessibility are becoming equally important. Stable‑coin flows that peak outside banking hours prove that market participants are already operating on a 24/7 rhythm. Token‑based settlement layers, when anchored to fiat‑backed stablecoins and central‑bank money, promise to retain the safety of the old system while adding the efficiency of the new. However, the transition hinges on two factors: **regulatory clarity** (to assure institutional investors that tokenised assets are not “unregulated junk”) and **issuer participation** (to provide the underlying securities that give the tokens real economic value).

### What to Watch  

- **SEC rule‑making timeline** – The SEC is expected to publish its final guidance on token classification and airdrop treatment by Q1 2027. The language will determine whether tokenised securities can be listed on existing exchanges or need a separate regulatory sandbox.  

- **South Korea’s stable‑coin framework** – The Korean Financial Services Commission is slated to release draft regulations for local‑currency stablecoins in November 2026. Adoption of KRW1 will depend on how quickly the framework is finalized and whether banks are allowed to hold the token on their balance sheets.  

- **Issuer opt‑out trends** – Monitor quarterly filings from U.S. public companies for statements about participation in tokenised‑stock platforms. A rise in opt‑outs could force platforms to redesign their governance models or focus on niche asset classes (e.g., private equity, real‑estate tokens).  

- **Adoption metrics from U.S. banks** – Coinbase’s “stable‑coin transfer” data will be released monthly. A sustained growth rate above 20% month‑over‑month would signal that banks are moving from pilot projects to core‑cash‑management functions.  

- **Malaysia’s DLT bond platform performance** – Track issuance volume, settlement times, and secondary‑market liquidity for tokenised sukuk on the RENTAS+‑linked ledger. Early success could inspire other central banks in the region to adopt similar infrastructure.
