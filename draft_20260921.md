**Headline**  
Binance re‑affirms EU push, US regulators tighten crypto oversight, and stable‑coin payments gain new capital – a week that reshapes the competitive map for banks, exchanges and treasury teams.

---

### What Happened  

**Binance brushes off speculation on ECB involvement** – The world’s largest crypto exchange told reporters it is “committed to securing authorization in Europe” after a report suggested the European Central Bank had intervened in Binance’s bid to obtain a MiCA licence in Greece. Binance declined to comment on the alleged ECB role, emphasizing instead that it is pursuing the formal registration process required under the EU’s Markets in Crypto‑Assets (MiCA) framework. (CoinTelegraph)

**Banks double their share on the EU MiCA provider register** – Data from the European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA) show that banks now account for roughly 23 % of entities listed on the MiCA register, up from about 12 % in late June. The surge reflects a wave of traditional financial institutions filing for crypto‑service licences, ranging from custodial solutions to token‑settlement platforms. (CoinTelegraph)

**Stable‑coin payments firm dtcpay raises $25 million** – Japan’s SBI Group led a Series A round that gave dtcpay a $25 million war chest. The company plans to broaden its merchant network and launch new payment products that settle in stablecoins, positioning itself as a bridge between Japanese retailers and the global digital‑currency ecosystem. (CoinTelegraph)

**Hyperliquid’s HYPE token spikes above $90** – After Hyperliquid introduced a manual borrowing feature that lets users take stable‑coin loans against HYPE or Bitcoin collateral, the HYPE token hit an all‑time high of $90.92. The move is intended to increase on‑chain liquidity and attract leveraged traders to the platform. (CoinTelegraph)

**Bitcoin climbs to $81 k amid US bond‑yield bounce** – Bitcoin rallied to $81,000 as U.S. 30‑year Treasury yields rose again, driven by concerns over global oil supply disruptions. The price move underscores the cryptocurrency’s continued sensitivity to macro‑financial variables. (CoinTelegraph)

**CFTC submits a crypto‑market regulatory plan to the White House** – The U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) delivered a comprehensive regulatory proposal to the White House for review, just days after the Senate failed to pass the CLARITY Act. The plan outlines how the agency will use its existing authority to oversee derivatives, spot markets and custodial services. (CoinTelegraph)

**Crypto‑linked equities rebound** – Stocks of Coinbase, Strategy Shares and other crypto‑related firms recovered after a brief sell‑off triggered by the stalled CLARITY legislation. The rally came as the CFTC and SEC signaled they would continue enforcement actions under existing rules rather than wait for new legislation. (CoinTelegraph)

**Coinbase files for single‑stock perpetual futures in the U.S.** – Coinbase submitted an application to launch 24‑hour/5‑day perpetual futures contracts on individual U.S. equities. The product would sit outside traditional exchange‑traded futures and would require approval from the CFTC and the SEC before it can be offered to retail and institutional traders. (CoinTelegraph)

### The Bigger Picture  

**Rebuilding the rails: regulation, liquidity and distribution**  
All of the headlines point to three intersecting “rails” that are being reconstructed: (1) the regulatory framework governing crypto activities, (2) the sources of on‑chain liquidity, and (3) the channels that move fiat and digital value between merchants, investors and custodians.  

First, the EU’s MiCA regime is moving from a paper‑only concept to a functional licensing system. Binance’s public denial of ECB meddling and the surge of banks on the MiCA register show that both crypto‑native firms and traditional players are scrambling to be first‑movers. The “rail” of regulatory legitimacy is being laid brick by brick, and whoever secures a licence early will gain a de‑facto market‑access passport across the 27‑member bloc.

Second, liquidity is being deepened through product innovation. Hyperliquid’s manual borrowing feature and Coinbase’s push for single‑stock perpetual futures illustrate a shift from pure spot trading to leveraged, on‑chain credit products. By allowing users to borrow stablecoins against crypto collateral, platforms are creating a new source of capital that does not rely on traditional bank funding. This expands the pool of traders who can participate, but it also introduces credit‑risk considerations that treasury teams must now monitor.

Third, distribution is widening beyond the crypto‑exchange ecosystem. dtcpay’s $25 million raise, backed by SBI, signals a strategic push to embed stable‑coin settlement into everyday merchant payments, especially in markets like Japan where cash still dominates. If successful, the stable‑coin rail could become a low‑cost, near‑instant alternative to domestic card‑network fees, giving corporates a new hedging tool against currency volatility.

**Winners, losers and the old‑new system balance**  

*Winners* – Traditional banks that secure MiCA licences are positioning themselves as custodians and service providers for a growing pool of institutional crypto demand. Their early entry gives them the credibility to partner with fintechs, offer white‑label solutions, and capture fee income that previously went to pure‑play exchanges. Likewise, exchanges that expand into leveraged on‑chain credit (e.g., Hyperliquid, Coinbase) stand to capture higher‑margin trading revenue and attract sophisticated retail traders looking for perpetual exposure without a broker‑dealer relationship.

*Losers* – Legacy payment processors and card networks could see incremental erosion of merchant volume if stable‑coin settlement gains traction, especially in regions where regulatory clarity makes crypto payments low‑risk. Moreover, crypto‑only firms that lack a regulated foothold in the EU may find themselves excluded from the continent’s $1 trillion‑plus digital‑asset market, limiting growth to less‑regulated jurisdictions.

*Systemic shift* – The old system—centralized, bank‑driven payments and regulated securities exchanges—remains dominant, but the emerging rails are creating a hybrid architecture. Banks are becoming “crypto‑service providers,” exchanges are morphing into “credit platforms,” and fintechs like dtcpay act as the distribution layer that connects merchants directly to stable‑coin liquidity. The result is a more fragmented but potentially more efficient ecosystem, where the competitive advantage hinges on regulatory compliance, credit‑risk management, and the ability to integrate fiat‑stable‑coin conversion at scale.
