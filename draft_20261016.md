**Headline**  
Regulators, payment giants and blockchain projects move in lockstep: Thailand’s crypto ETFs launch, Russia’s P2P ban, Mastercard’s stable‑coin bridge and a quantum‑risk debate reshape the finance‑crypto frontier.

---

### What Happened  

- **Thailand’s first Bitcoin and Ether ETFs go live** – The Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) approved the new rules on Friday, allowing the country’s first crypto‑linked exchange‑traded funds to be listed. CoinShares, a major asset manager, warned that the inflows will be modest at first because investors already have access to offshore products and local crypto‑focused trusts. (CoinTelegraph)

- **Starknet asks its community to vote on an exit from Ethereum** – The layer‑2 scaling solution is asking STRK token holders whether it should become an independent layer‑1 blockchain. The move is driven by concerns that Ethereum’s upcoming post‑quantum security upgrade may be delayed, leaving Starknet exposed to future quantum‑computing attacks. (CoinTelegraph)

- **Russia will ban direct peer‑to‑peer crypto trades from July 2027** – The new regulation will prohibit individuals from matching buyers and sellers on‑chain without an intermediary. Existing rules already forbid moving funds from digital‑account wallets to self‑custody wallets, tightening the state’s grip on retail crypto activity. (CoinTelegraph)

- **Mastercard launches the Global Money Platform** – The payments network’s first major stable‑coin integration, built on the BVNK acquisition, lets banks and card issuers hold and move stable‑coin balances alongside fiat. Early adopters include Nuvei and Marqeta, signaling a push to bring crypto liquidity into everyday payment flows. (CoinTelegraph)

- **Sweden revisits a digital krona as the ECB pushes a digital euro** – The Riksbank’s latest recommendation urges lawmakers to prepare legislation for a central‑bank digital currency (CBDC). The drive is motivated by worries that foreign‑currency stablecoins could erode the domestic payments ecosystem. (CoinTelegraph)

- **A former Trump crypto adviser profits from a stable‑coin bill** – The Wall Street Journal, cited by CoinTelegraph, reported that Bo Hines, who advised the former president on crypto policy, earned sizable gains after the passage of the GENIUS stable‑coin legislation, raising questions about lobbying influence. (CoinTelegraph)

- **Binance.US seeks a $1 billion valuation in a $100 million raise** – The U.S. arm of the global exchange is courting investors to fund a round that would value the business at $1 bn, a step aimed at recapturing market share after a 2023 settlement with U.S. regulators. (CoinTelegraph)

- **Scammer loses $4.8 million stored on tampered Ledger devices** – A fraudster who stole crypto from victims attempted to keep the proceeds on Ledger hardware wallets that had been compromised. The loss underscores ongoing supply‑chain and device‑security risks. (CoinTelegraph)

### The Bigger Picture  

All of these stories point to a single, accelerating shift: **the rail on which capital moves is being rebuilt from a fiat‑centric, opaque network to a hybrid of regulated digital assets and traditional finance.**  

1. **Regulators are tightening the doors while also opening new ones.**  
   - Thailand’s cautious rollout of crypto ETFs shows a “sandbox‑to‑mainstream” approach—allowing regulated exposure to Bitcoin and Ether while keeping the broader market under oversight.  
   - Russia’s upcoming P2P ban and Sweden’s CBDC push illustrate two opposite tactics: one blocks retail crypto activity outright, the other creates a state‑issued digital alternative to pre‑empt private stablecoins.  
   - The United States continues to enforce compliance, as seen in Binance.US’s need for fresh capital to satisfy regulators and rebuild credibility.

2. **Infrastructure providers are bridging the gap.**  
   - Mastercard’s Global Money Platform is a concrete example of a legacy payments player embedding stable‑coin balances into its existing card and banking stack. By doing so, it reduces friction for institutions that want crypto exposure without building a separate ledger.  
   - Starknet’s quantum‑risk debate highlights that the technical foundations of many DeFi projects are still vulnerable. If a major L2 decides to become an independent L1, it could create a new “crypto rail” that bypasses Ethereum’s security roadmap, potentially fragmenting liquidity.

3. **Who wins, who loses?**  
   - **Winners:** Asset managers (e.g., CoinShares) that can package crypto exposure in familiar ETF structures; payment processors that add stable‑coin capabilities; jurisdictions that design clear, supportive frameworks (Thailand, potentially Sweden).  
   - **Losers:** Retail participants in restrictive markets (Russia) and projects that rely on a single‑point security upgrade (Starknet) if they cannot adapt quickly. Scammers and the illicit ecosystem also face higher operational risk as hardware security improves and custodial solutions become more regulated.  

4. **Implications for the old vs. new system**  
   - The **old system**—bank‑centric, ledger‑free, and regulated by national authorities—remains the backbone for large‑scale payments and sovereign money. Yet it is increasingly forced to accommodate digital‑asset flows or risk losing relevance.  
   - The **new system**—a patchwork of regulated ETFs, stable‑coin bridges, and emerging CBDCs—offers faster settlement, programmable money, and new yield opportunities, but it still depends on clear regulatory sign‑offs and robust security. The coexistence of both rails will likely persist for several years, with the balance shifting toward the hybrid model as more institutions adopt crypto‑compatible infrastructure.
